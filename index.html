Three-Dimensional Coordinate Systems

Everything in Calc III lives in space instead of on a plane, so the first job is just getting comfortable with a third axis.

To locate a point in space we use three mutually perpendicular axes — x, y, and z — meeting at the origin O. A point is written P(a, b, c), found by moving a units along x, b along y, and c along z. Stewart's textbook uses a right-handed coordinate system: curl the fingers of your right hand from the positive x-axis toward the positive y-axis, and your thumb points along the positive z-axis. If you used your left hand instead you'd get a mirror-image (left-handed) system — the convention matters later for cross products and orientation, so it's worth building the right-hand habit now.

The three coordinate axes determine three coordinate planes: the xy-plane (z = 0), the yz-plane (x = 0), and the xz-plane (y = 0). These divide space into eight octants, the first of which is where x, y, z are all positive — the 3D analogue of "Quadrant I." A useful mental model: imagine standing in the corner of a room where the floor and two walls meet. The floor is the xy-plane, and the two walls are the xz- and yz-planes; the corner itself is the origin.

Two things trip people up early in this section. First, every equation you write in ℝ³ describes a region of space, not just a set of numbers — an equation like x = 5 doesn't mean "one point," it means "every point where x is 5, no matter what y and z are," which is an entire plane parallel to the yz-plane. Second, projections: the projection of a point P(a,b,c) onto the xy-plane is (a,b,0) — you literally set the missing coordinate to zero and keep the other two. This idea of projecting onto a plane by dropping a coordinate comes back constantly in Chapter 15 when you set up bounds for double and triple integrals.

Reading coordinate-plane and coordinate-axis equations
x = k is a plane parallel to the yz-plane. y = k is a plane parallel to the xz-plane. z = k is a plane parallel to the xy-plane. If two variables are fixed (like x=2, y=3 together) you get a line parallel to the z-axis. This "one equation removes one degree of freedom" idea is the same logic used later for cylinders in 12.6.

Distance and spheres

Distance formula in ℝ³
|P₁P₂| = √[(x₂−x₁)² + (y₂−y₁)² + (z₂−z₁)²]
Equation of a sphere
(x − h)² + (y − k)² + (z − l)² = r²
Center (h, k, l), radius r — the 3D analogue of a circle. If you expand a quadratic equation in x, y, z and it doesn't obviously match this form, complete the square in each variable to find the center and radius.

Where does the sphere equation come from? A sphere is, by definition, the set of every point whose distance from a fixed center (h,k,l) equals a fixed radius r. So you literally start from the distance formula, |P − center| = r, and square both sides to clear the square root — that's the entire derivation. This is the same pattern you'll see over and over in this course: a geometric definition (all points satisfying some distance/angle condition) turns directly into an algebraic equation.

Completing the square, step by step: if you're given something like x² + bx, you want to turn it into (x + b/2)² by adding and subtracting (b/2)². The two-line habit that keeps this error-free: (1) take half the coefficient on the linear term, square it; (2) add that number inside the group, and immediately subtract the same number outside (or add it to the other side) so the equation stays balanced. Do this once for each of x, y, z separately — the terms don't interact with each other.

Worked Example
Show that x² + y² + z² + 4x − 6y + 2z = 6 represents a sphere, and find its center and radius.
1
Group by variable and complete the square in each:
(x² + 4x) + (y² − 6y) + (z² + 2z) = 6
2
Complete each square (add the same constants to both sides):
(x+2)² − 4 + (y−3)² − 9 + (z+1)² − 1 = 6
3
Combine constants:
(x+2)² + (y−3)² + (z+1)² = 6 + 4 + 9 + 1 = 20
Center (−2, 3, −1), radius √20 = 2√5.
Reading a region description
An equation involving only two of the three variables (like x² + y² = 9 in ℝ³) is not a curve — it's a cylinder, since z is unrestricted. You'll formalize this in 12.6, but it's worth noticing now.

Vectors

A vector carries both magnitude and direction — think displacement, velocity, or force, as opposed to a plain number.

Geometrically, a vector is a directed line segment; two segments represent the same vector if they have the same length and direction, regardless of where they're drawn. This "free-floating" property — a vector doesn't care where it starts, only how far and which way it points — is the single biggest conceptual difference between a vector and a point, and it's what makes vectors so useful: you can slide the exact same displacement vector all over a diagram without changing what it represents.

Algebraically, we place a vector's tail at the origin and record its tip's coordinates: a = ⟨a₁, a₂, a₃⟩. This particular placement (tail at the origin) is called the position vector — it's the "standard" representative of a whole equivalence class of directed segments that all share the same length and direction. If A(x₁,y₁,z₁) and B(x₂,y₂,z₂) are any two points, the vector from A to B has components ⟨x₂−x₁, y₂−y₁, z₂−z₁⟩ — you're finding "how far you'd have to walk in each direction" to get from A to B, which is exactly tip-minus-tail, or terminal-point-minus-initial-point. Getting this order right (terminal minus initial, not the other way around) is one of the most common sources of sign errors in this chapter.

It helps to keep two mental pictures side by side: the Triangle Law (place the tail of the second vector at the tip of the first; the sum runs from the start of the first to the end of the second) and the Parallelogram Law (place both vectors' tails at the same point; the sum is the diagonal of the parallelogram they form). They describe the same operation — pick whichever picture makes a given problem easier to visualize.

Operations

Magnitude
|a| = √(a₁² + a₂² + a₃²)
Addition, subtraction, scalar multiplication
a + b = ⟨a₁+b₁, a₂+b₂, a₃+b₃⟩
a − b = ⟨a₁−b₁, a₂−b₂, a₃−b₃⟩
ca = ⟨ca₁, ca₂, ca₃⟩
Add/subtract component-by-component; scale every component by c.
Standard basis vectors
i = ⟨1,0,0⟩   j = ⟨0,1,0⟩   k = ⟨0,0,1⟩
a = a₁i + a₂j + a₃k
Unit vector in the direction of a
û = a / |a|
Divide by the magnitude to get a vector of length 1 pointing the same way.

Why does dividing by |a| always produce a length-1 vector? Because scalar multiplication scales the length by exactly the factor you multiply by: |ca| = |c||a|. So if you multiply a by the scalar 1/|a|, the new length is (1/|a|)·|a| = 1, no matter what direction a originally pointed. This little fact is used constantly for the rest of the course — anytime you need "a vector of length 1 pointing this way," you build it by dividing that vector by its own magnitude.

It's worth pausing on why the eight algebraic properties listed above (commutativity, associativity, distributivity, etc.) matter: they're exactly the properties that let you manipulate vector expressions the same way you manipulate ordinary algebra — combining like terms, factoring out scalars, and so on — as long as you never try to "divide by a vector" or multiply two vectors together carelessly (dot and cross products, coming in 12.3 and 12.4, have their own special rules).

Worked Example
Let a = ⟨4, 0, 3⟩ and b = ⟨−2, 1, 5⟩. Find |a|, a + b, a − 2b, and a unit vector in the direction of a.
1

2

a + b = ⟨4−2, 0+1, 3+5⟩ = ⟨2, 1, 8⟩
3
Scale then subtract:
a − 2b = ⟨4,0,3⟩ − ⟨−4,2,10⟩ = ⟨8, −2, −7⟩
4

|a| = 5, a+b = ⟨2,1,8⟩, a−2b = ⟨8,−2,−7⟩, û = ⟨4/5, 0, 3/5⟩
Vector vs. point
⟨3, 2⟩ as a vector and (3, 2) as a point are different objects even though they share the same numbers — the angle-bracket notation exists specifically to keep them apart. Watch for this on quizzes.

The Dot Product

The dot product turns two vectors into a single number that measures how much they point the same way — it's the key to angles, projections, and (later) work.

Notice something important: the dot product takes two vectors and returns a plain number (a scalar) — this is why it's also called the scalar product. That's fundamentally different from the cross product you'll meet next, which takes two vectors and returns another vector. Keeping "dot → number" and "cross → vector" straight from day one will save you from a lot of confused answers later, especially once both operations start appearing in the same problem (as they do in 12.4's scalar triple product, and constantly throughout Chapter 16).

The two formulas — the algebraic one (multiply matching components, add them up) and the geometric one (|a||b|cos θ) — describe the exact same number; Stewart's text proves this using the Law of Cosines on the triangle formed by a, b, and a−b. That's worth remembering as a proof technique in its own right, since the same triangle trick reappears in a few other derivations. In practice, you'll use the algebraic formula whenever you're handed components, and the geometric formula (solved for cos θ) whenever you need to extract an angle.

Algebraic definition
a · b = a₁b₁ + a₂b₂ + a₃b₃
Geometric interpretation
a · b = |a||b| cos θ
θ is the angle between a and b, 0 ≤ θ ≤ π. Solve for cos θ to find the angle between two vectors.
Orthogonality test
a ⊥ b  ⟺  a · b = 0
Scalar and vector projections of b onto a
compab = (a · b)/|a|    projab = [(a · b)/|a|²] a
comp is the signed length of b's "shadow" on a; proj is that shadow as an actual vector along a.

Direction angles α, β, γ are the angles a vector makes with the positive x, y, z axes; cos α = a₁/|a|, and similarly for β, γ. These come directly from the geometric dot-product formula applied to a and each standard basis vector: a · i = |a||i| cos α = |a| cos α, but a · i is also just a₁ by the algebraic formula — set those equal and solve for cos α. These direction cosines satisfy cos²α + cos²β + cos²γ = 1 (this is really just |a|² = a₁²+a₂²+a₃² in disguise, divided through by |a|²).

The projection formulas deserve a closer look because the two versions (scalar vs. vector) are easy to mix up. Picture shining a flashlight straight down onto vector a from directly above b — the "shadow" b casts onto the line through a is the projection. compab is just the signed length of that shadow (positive if the shadow points the same way as a, negative if it points backward). projab takes that same shadow and expresses it as an actual vector lying along a — you get it by taking the unit vector â = a/|a| and scaling it by compab: projab = (compab) â.

Worked Example
Find the angle between a = ⟨2, 2, −1⟩ and b = ⟨5, −3, 2⟩, and the scalar projection of b onto a.
1
Dot product:
a·b = 10 − 6 − 2 = 2
2
Magnitudes:
|a| = √(4+4+1) = 3,   |b| = √(25+9+4) = √38
3
Solve cos θ = (a·b)/(|a||b|):
cos θ = 2/(3√38) ⇒ θ = cos⁻¹(2/(3√38)) ≈ 83.9°
4
Scalar projection:
compab = (a·b)/|a| = 2/3
θ ≈ 83.9°, compab = 2/3
comp vs. proj
compab is just a number (can be negative if the angle is obtuse). projab is a vector — multiply compab by the unit vector â to get it: projab = (compab) â.
Worked Example 2 — Work as a dot product
A constant force F = ⟨2, 3, 1⟩ (in Newtons) moves an object along the straight-line displacement D = ⟨4, 0, 3⟩ (in meters). Find the work done.
1
Physically, work is force times distance in the direction of motion — exactly what the dot product computes:
W = F · D
2
Compute the dot product directly:
W = (2)(4) + (3)(0) + (1)(3) = 8 + 0 + 3
W = 11 joules. (This is exactly why the dot product is defined the way it is — |F||D|cosθ is "the part of the force that actually pushes along the direction of travel," times the distance traveled.)

The Cross Product

Unlike the dot product, the cross product of two vectors in ℝ³ produces a vector — one that's perpendicular to both.

The cross product only makes sense in three dimensions (this is different from the dot product, which is perfectly well defined in any number of dimensions). Given two vectors a and b that aren't parallel, there are exactly two directions perpendicular to both of them — straight "up" out of the plane they span, or straight "down." The right-hand rule resolves this ambiguity: point the fingers of your right hand along a, curl them toward b, and your thumb points in the direction of a × b.

The determinant formula looks intimidating the first time you see it, but it's really just a bookkeeping device — expanding a symbolic 3×3 determinant along the top row automatically produces the correct component formula with the correct signs. Watch the alternating signs carefully: the coefficient of j comes with a minus sign in front of it, which is where most students lose points.

Determinant formula
a × b = | i  j  k ; a₁ a₂ a₃ ; b₁ b₂ b₃ |
= (a₂b₃ − a₃b₂) i − (a₁b₃ − a₃b₁) j + (a₁b₂ − a₂b₁) k
Expand the 3×3 determinant along the top row.
Geometric interpretation
|a × b| = |a||b| sin θ
Direction: perpendicular to both a and b, given by the right-hand rule. |a × b| also equals the area of the parallelogram spanned by a and b.
Parallel test & scalar triple product
a ∥ b  ⟺  a × b = 0
a · (b × c) = volume of parallelepiped spanned by a, b, c
Order matters
The cross product is anti-commutative: b × a = −(a × b). Flipping the order reverses the direction of the result. It is also not associative: (a×b)×c generally does not equal a×(b×c).

Why does |a × b| equal the parallelogram's area? Recall from geometry that a parallelogram's area is base × height. If you treat a as the base (length |a|) and drop a perpendicular from the tip of b down to the line through a, that perpendicular height works out to |b| sin θ (basic right-triangle trig, where θ is the angle between a and b). Multiply base times height: |a| · |b| sin θ — which is exactly the geometric formula for |a × b|. The scalar triple product a · (b × c) extends this one dimension further: it gives the (signed) volume of the parallelepiped (a 3D "slanted box") with edges a, b, c. You'll use this to test whether three vectors are coplanar — if a · (b × c) = 0, the parallelepiped is flat, so the three vectors all lie in the same plane.

Equations of Lines and Planes

A line needs a point and a direction; a plane needs a point and a normal vector. Everything else follows from those two facts.

This section is really about translating between two ways of thinking about the same object. A line in space is completely determined once you know one point it passes through and the direction it travels in — no different from 2D, except the direction vector now has three components. A plane, on the other hand, is completely determined by one point on it and a vector perpendicular to it (the normal vector) — this is a genuinely 3D idea, since "perpendicular to a whole plane" doesn't exist as a concept in 2D.

Vector equation
r = r₀ + t v
r₀ is the position vector of a known point on the line, v = ⟨a,b,c⟩ is a direction vector, t ranges over ℝ.
Parametric equations
x = x₀ + at   y = y₀ + bt   z = z₀ + ct
Symmetric equations
(x−x₀)/a = (y−y₀)/b = (z−z₀)/c
Valid when a, b, c are all nonzero — solve each parametric equation for t and set them equal.

Planes

Vector / scalar equation
n · (r − r₀) = 0
a(x−x₀) + b(y−y₀) + c(z−z₀) = 0
n = ⟨a,b,c⟩ is a normal vector to the plane, (x₀,y₀,z₀) a known point on it.
Linear equation
ax + by + cz = d
Distance from point P₁ to plane ax+by+cz=d
D = |a x₁ + b y₁ + c z₁ − d| / √(a²+b²+c²)
Finding a normal vector
Given three points in a plane, form two vectors between them and take their cross product — that gives you n. Given two lines or two direction vectors that lie in the plane, cross those instead.

Where the plane equation comes from: if r₀ is a known point in the plane and r = ⟨x,y,z⟩ is any other point in the plane, the vector r − r₀ lies entirely within the plane — which means it must be perpendicular to the normal vector n. "Perpendicular" is exactly what a dot product of zero tests for, which is why n · (r−r₀) = 0 is the defining equation. Expand that dot product component-by-component and you get the familiar a(x−x₀)+b(y−y₀)+c(z−z₀)=0, which simplifies to ax+by+cz=d once you collect the constants (with d = ax₀+by₀+cz₀).

The distance-from-a-point-to-a-plane formula follows the same logic as scalar projection from 12.3: pick any point Q on the plane, form the vector from Q to your point P₁, and project that vector onto the (unit) normal direction. The absolute value of that projection is the perpendicular distance — which is exactly what the formula D = |ax₁+by₁+cz₁−d| / √(a²+b²+c²) computes.

Cylinders and Quadric Surfaces

The last piece of the geometric toolkit: recognizing common 3D surfaces from their equations, mostly by their traces.

A cylinder in the Calc III sense is any surface generated by moving a straight line (parallel to a fixed axis) along a plane curve. If an equation is missing one variable entirely, it's a cylinder — the missing variable is the one the surface extends infinitely along. For example x² + y² = 9 (no z) is a circular cylinder extending along the z-axis.

A quadric surface is the graph of a second-degree equation in x, y, z. To identify one, find its traces — the curves obtained by setting x, y, or z equal to a constant — usually z = k. This is genuinely the only reliable strategy: rather than trying to memorize what every possible surface looks like from its equation alone, you slice it with a few well-chosen planes and identify each resulting 2D curve (circle, ellipse, parabola, hyperbola, or a pair of lines), then mentally stack those slices back together.

A practical workflow that works for almost every quadric surface problem you'll see: (1) if the equation isn't already in the "=1" or "=0" standard form, divide through to get there; (2) set z = k and simplify — this is usually the most informative trace; (3) also check x = k and y = k if the surface isn't obviously symmetric; (4) match the pattern of signs against the six standard forms below. Once you can recognize these six by their sign pattern, identifying a quadric becomes almost mechanical.

Ellipsoid
x²/a² + y²/b² + z²/c² = 1
All traces are ellipses (or circles if two denominators match).
Elliptic paraboloid
z/c = x²/a² + y²/b²
Horizontal traces (z = k) are ellipses; vertical traces are parabolas. Bowl-shaped.
Hyperbolic paraboloid (saddle)
z/c = x²/a² − y²/b²
Horizontal traces are hyperbolas; vertical traces are parabolas, opening opposite ways in x vs. y.
Cone
z²/c² = x²/a² + y²/b²
Hyperboloid of one sheet
x²/a² + y²/b² − z²/c² = 1
Connected surface with an elliptical "waist."
Hyperboloid of two sheets
−x²/a² − y²/b² + z²/c² = 1
Two separate, disconnected pieces — note two minus signs vs. one for the one-sheet version.
The fastest way to identify a quadric
Count the negative (or non-squared) terms. All three variables squared with the same sign, equal to 1 → ellipsoid. One variable linear (not squared) → paraboloid. One minus sign among three squared terms set to 1 → hyperboloid of one sheet. Two minus signs → two sheets. Set equal to 0 → cone.
Worked Example
Identify the surface 4x² − y² + 2z² = 4, and describe its trace in the plane y = k.
1
Divide by 4 to get standard form:
x² − y²/4 + z²/2 = 1
2
Two positive squared terms (x², z²) and one negative (y²), all equal to 1 → hyperboloid of one sheet, with axis along the y-axis.
3
Trace at y = k: substitute and simplify:
x² + z²/2 = 1 + k²/4
Hyperboloid of one sheet (axis = y-axis); every trace y = k is an ellipse that grows as |k| increases.
Worked Example 2 — Hyperbolic paraboloid
Identify z = y² − x², and describe the traces at z=0, z=1, and x=0.
1
Rewrite as z = y²/1 − x²/1: this matches z/c = y²/b² − x²/a² (roles of x,y swapped from the standard box), so it's a hyperbolic paraboloid (saddle).
2
Trace at z=0:
y² − x² = 0 ⟹ y = ±x
— two crossing lines, the "center" of the saddle.
3
Trace at z=1:
y² − x² = 1
— a hyperbola opening along the y-axis.
4
Trace at x=0 (a vertical slice):
z = y²
— an upward-opening parabola.
Hyperbolic paraboloid; horizontal traces are hyperbolas (or crossing lines at z=0), vertical traces are parabolas — this is the classic "saddle" or "Pringle chip" shape.

Vector Functions and Space Curves

Instead of tracking one output, a vector function tracks a whole point in space as it moves — this is how we describe curves and motion.

A vector-valued function r(t) assigns a vector to each value of t: r(t) = ⟨f(t), g(t), h(t)⟩ = f(t)i + g(t)j + h(t)k. As t varies, the tip of r(t) traces out a space curve C. The domain of r is the intersection of the domains of f, g, h — you need every component function to be defined simultaneously, so find each piece's individual domain restriction and intersect them, exactly like example 1 below.

It helps to think of t as time and r(t) as the position of a moving particle. This isn't just a metaphor — it's literally the setup used throughout this chapter and again in physics: r(t) traces the path, r′(t) (next section) will give velocity, and r″(t) will give acceleration. A single vector function packages what would otherwise be three separate single-variable functions (x(t), y(t), z(t)) into one object you can differentiate and integrate all at once.

Limit of a vector function
lim r(t) = ⟨lim f(t), lim g(t), lim h(t)⟩
Take the limit componentwise. r is continuous at a if this limit equals r(a).
Common parametrizations to recognize
Helix: ⟨a cos t, a sin t, ct⟩ (circular motion in x-y, steadily rising in z). Line: r₀ + tv. Intersection of two surfaces: solve for two variables in terms of a parameter using one equation, substitute into the other.
Worked Example
Sketch/describe the curve r(t) = ⟨cos t, sin t, t⟩, and find where it intersects the plane z = π.
1
The x, y components satisfy x²+y² = cos²t+sin²t = 1 for every t — so the curve always lies on the cylinder x²+y²=1.
2
As t increases, z = t climbs steadily while (x,y) circles the unit circle — this is a helix.
3
Set z = t = π:
r(π) = ⟨cos π, sin π, π⟩ = ⟨−1, 0, π⟩
A helix on the cylinder x²+y²=1; it meets z = π at the point (−1, 0, π).
Worked Example 2 — Curve of intersection

1
One variable is already pinned down by the plane: y = 1 everywhere on this curve, so let x be the free parameter: x = t.

Substitute x=t, y=1 into the paraboloid equation to get z in terms of t:
z = t² + 1² = t² + 1
r(t) = ⟨t, 1, t²+1⟩, t ∈ ℝ — this describes a parabola sitting in the plane y=1.

Derivatives and Integrals of Vector Functions

Differentiate and integrate a vector function exactly the way you'd expect — one component at a time.

Geometrically, r′(t) is defined the same way an ordinary derivative is — as the limit of a difference quotient, [r(t+h) − r(t)]/h — except now the numerator is a vector (the secant line connecting two nearby points on the curve) instead of a number. As h → 0, that secant vector rotates into the tangent vector at r(t). This is why r′(t) always points along the curve's direction of travel: it's built from displacement, not just a slope.

All five differentiation rules in the box below look exactly like the ordinary product rule and sum rule from Calc I — because in a real sense, they are the product/sum rule, just applied component by component and then reassembled. The two that need the most care are the last two: since u·v is a number, differentiating it uses the ordinary scalar product rule; since u×v is a vector, you must preserve the order of u and v in each term (recall b×a = −a×b), so u′×v + u×v′ is correct but v′×u + v×u′ would flip signs incorrectly.

Derivative
r′(t) = ⟨f′(t), g′(t), h′(t)⟩
r′(t) is tangent to the curve at r(t), and points in the direction of increasing t. If r′(t) ≠ 0, the unit tangent vector is T(t) = r′(t)/|r′(t)|.
Differentiation rules
d/dt[u(t)+v(t)] = u′+v′
d/dt[cu(t)] = cu′(t)
d/dt[f(t)u(t)] = f′(t)u(t)+f(t)u′(t)
d/dt[u·v] = u′·v + u·v′
d/dt[u×v] = u′×v + u×v′
Product-rule style — but keep dot vs. cross products, and order (for cross), exactly as written.
Integral
∫r(t) dt = ⟨∫f(t)dt, ∫g(t)dt, ∫h(t)dt⟩ + C
C here is a constant vector.
Worked Example

1

2
Evaluate at t = 0:
r′(0) = ⟨0, 1, 1⟩
3
Normalize:
|r′(0)| = √2 ⇒ T(0) = ⟨0, 1/√2, 1/√2⟩
r′(t) = ⟨2t, cos t, e^t⟩; T(0) = ⟨0, 1/√2, 1/√2⟩
Worked Example 2 — Finding r(t) from r′(t)
Find r(t) given r′(t) = ⟨2t, 3t², 1⟩ and the initial condition r(0) = ⟨1, 0, 5⟩.

Plug in t=0 and match to the given initial condition ⟨1,0,5⟩:
r(0) = ⟨C₁, C₂, C₃⟩ = ⟨1, 0, 5⟩
r(t) = ⟨t²+1, t³, t+5⟩

Arc Length and Curvature

Two natural questions about a curve: how long is it, and how sharply does it bend? Both have clean formulas in terms of r(t).

The arc length formula is really just an extension of the Calc II formula L = ∫√(1+(dy/dx)²)dx — that formula is the special case of the general L = ∫|r′(t)|dt when the curve is parametrized as r(t) = ⟨t, f(t)⟩. |r′(t)| gives the instantaneous "speed" of the parametrization (how fast the point is tracing the curve), so integrating speed over the time interval gives total distance traveled — exactly the same logic as ∫speed dt = distance from a physics course.

Curvature is a subtler idea worth sitting with: T(t), the unit tangent vector, always has length 1 — it only ever changes direction, never magnitude. So |T′(t)| measures purely how fast the direction is rotating, which is exactly "how sharply the curve bends." Dividing by |r′(t)| converts this from "rate of turning per unit of the parameter t" into "rate of turning per unit of actual arc length traveled" — which is what makes κ a genuine geometric property of the curve, independent of how fast you happen to be parametrizing it.

Arc length
L = ∫ₐᵇ |r′(t)| dt = ∫ₐᵇ √[(x′)² + (y′)² + (z′)²] dt
Arc length function
s(t) = ∫ₐᵗ |r′(u)| du    ds/dt = |r′(t)|
Measures length traveled from t = a up to the current t; used to reparametrize by arc length.
Curvature
κ(t) = |T′(t)| / |r′(t)|  =  |r′(t) × r″(t)| / |r′(t)|³
The second (determinant) form is usually faster — no need to normalize r′ first. For y = f(x): κ = |f″(x)| / [1+(f′(x))²]^(3/2).
Unit normal & binormal
N(t) = T′(t)/|T′(t)|    B(t) = T(t) × N(t)
T, N, B form a moving right-handed frame along the curve (the TNB / Frenet frame).
Worked Example
Find the curvature of the helix r(t) = ⟨3 sin t, 3 cos t, 4t⟩.
1
First and second derivatives:
r′(t) = ⟨3cos t, −3sin t, 4⟩   r″(t) = ⟨−3sin t, −3cos t, 0⟩
2

(−3sin t)(0) − (4)(−3cos t) = 12cos t
3
j and k components similarly give:
r′×r″ = ⟨12cos t, −12sin t, −9⟩,   |r′×r″| = √(144+81) = 15
4
|r′(t)| = √(9+16) = 5, so |r′|³ = 125. Then:
κ = 15/125 = 3/25
κ(t) = 3/25 (constant — makes sense for a helix of fixed radius/pitch)
Sanity check
Curvature of a straight line is always 0; curvature of a circle of radius r is the constant 1/r. Use these to check your formula setup.
Worked Example 2 — Curvature of a circle (sanity check)

Functions of Several Variables

This is where Calc III really begins: functions that take in more than one input, like z = f(x, y), a surface instead of a curve.

A function of two variables assigns a unique real number f(x,y) to each point (x,y) in some domain D ⊆ ℝ². Its graph is the surface {(x, y, f(x,y))} in ℝ³. Notice this is the exact same idea as a single-variable function's graph {(x, f(x))} — you're still plotting "input(s), then output" — you've just added a second input, which means the graph now needs a third dimension to be drawn. That's the whole conceptual leap of this chapter: everything you already know from Calc I (rates of change, tangent lines, max/min values) has a direct multivariable analogue; the machinery just has one more variable to track.

To visualize such a surface without a 3D plot, use level curves: the curve f(x,y) = k, for various constants k, drawn in the xy-plane — this is exactly how a topographic (contour) map works, where each curve connects all the points at the same elevation. For functions of three variables w = f(x,y,z), the analogous tool is a level surface f(x,y,z) = k (you can't draw a 4D graph, but you absolutely can draw the 3D surface where the function has a fixed value — this is exactly how the tangent-plane-to-a-level-surface idea in 14.6 works).

Finding a domain in this chapter usually reduces to the same three checks you already know from single-variable functions, just applied to an expression in two (or three) variables instead of one: no dividing by zero, no even root of a negative number, no logarithm of a non-positive number. The only new wrinkle is that the resulting domain is now a region of the plane (or of space) — a disk, a half-plane, the area above a curve — rather than just an interval of the number line.

Reading level curves
Closely spaced level curves mean the surface is steep there (z changes fast over a short distance); widely spaced curves mean it's nearly flat.
Worked Example

Limits and Continuity

In one variable, x can only approach a from two directions. In the plane, (x,y) can approach (a,b) along infinitely many paths — and the limit only exists if every path gives the same answer.

Definition
lim(x,y)→(a,b) f(x,y) = L
means f(x,y) can be made arbitrarily close to L by taking (x,y) sufficiently close to (a,b), along any path of approach.
The two-path test (for proving a limit does NOT exist)
If f(x,y) → L₁ along one path (e.g. y = 0) and f(x,y) → L₂ ≠ L₁ along another (e.g. y = x), the limit does not exist. This only disproves a limit — matching along a few paths never proves it exists, since some other path could still disagree.

To actually prove a limit exists (when you suspect it does), bound |f(x,y) − L| by something that squeezes to 0 — often by switching to polar coordinates (x = r cos θ, y = r sin θ) and letting r → 0⁺, independent of θ. f is continuous at (a,b) if lim(x,y)→(a,b) f(x,y) = f(a,b); polynomials and rational functions (away from zero denominators) are continuous everywhere on their domain, just as in one variable.

Why does polar substitution work so well for these limits? Because (x,y) → (0,0) is exactly equivalent to r → 0⁺, no matter what direction θ you approach from. If, after substituting x = r cosθ and y = r sinθ, you can bound the whole expression by something like C·r (where C doesn't depend on θ at all — often because it involves only bounded factors like sinθ, cosθ, or sin θcos θ, all of which stay between −1 and 1), then by the Squeeze Theorem the limit must be 0 regardless of the angle of approach. If instead θ refuses to cancel out of the final bound, that's usually a sign the limit doesn't exist.

A key practical distinction: proving a limit doesn't exist only requires finding two paths that disagree — often y = 0, then y = mx (a general line through the origin, or y=x specifically), then a curve like y = x² if lines alone don't reveal a contradiction. But proving a limit does exist is a fundamentally stronger claim — no finite collection of paths, however cleverly chosen, can ever rule out every possible path of approach. That asymmetry is why the polar/squeeze technique (or an explicit inequality bound) is the only reliable way to actually establish that a limit exists.

Worked Example
Show that lim(x,y)→(0,0) (xy)/(x²+y²) does not exist.
1
Approach along y = 0 (the x-axis):
f(x,0) = 0/x² = 0 → 0
2
Approach along y = x:
f(x,x) = x²/(2x²) = 1/2 → 1/2
Different limits along different paths (0 ≠ 1/2), so the limit does not exist.
Worked Example 2 — Proving a limit exists
Show that lim(x,y)→(0,0) (3x²y)/(x²+y²) = 0.
1
Substitute polar coordinates x=r cosθ, y=r sinθ, so x²+y²=r²:
f = 3(r²cos²θ)(r sinθ)/r² = 3r cos²θ sinθ
2
Bound using |cosθ|≤1 and |sinθ|≤1:
|f| = 3r|cos²θ sinθ| ≤ 3r
3
As (x,y)→(0,0), r→0⁺, so 3r→0. Squeeze Theorem: since 0 ≤ |f| ≤ 3r and 3r→0, |f| is forced to 0 too — regardless of θ, which is exactly what "the limit exists" requires.
The limit equals 0 (no path-dependence survives, since the bound 3r had no θ left in it).

Partial Derivatives

To differentiate a multivariable function, freeze every variable but one and differentiate as usual — that's the whole idea.

Geometrically, fx(a,b) is the slope of the tangent line to the curve you get by slicing the surface z=f(x,y) with the vertical plane y=b — you're looking at how z changes as you move in the x-direction only, holding y fixed at b. Likewise fy(a,b) is the slope of the tangent line where the plane x=a slices the surface. This is why "hold the other variable constant" isn't just an algebra trick — it corresponds to a genuine geometric slice of the surface.

In practice, almost every partial-derivative computation is just an ordinary single-variable derivative in disguise: whichever variable you're not differentiating with respect to gets treated exactly like a fixed number (say, treat y as if it were literally "5"). All your familiar rules — power rule, product rule, quotient rule, chain rule — apply unchanged; the only new skill is remembering which letter is currently "frozen."

Definition
fx(a,b) = limh→0 [f(a+h,b) − f(a,b)] / h
fy(a,b) = limh→0 [f(a,b+h) − f(a,b)] / h
In practice: to find fx, treat y as a constant and differentiate with respect to x. Same idea for fy.
Notation
fx = ∂f/∂x = ∂z/∂x = f₁ = D₁f
Second-order partials & Clairaut's Theorem
fxy = ∂²f/∂y∂x    fyx = ∂²f/∂x∂y
If fxy and fyx are both continuous, they're equal — mixed partials commute. This is a huge shortcut and is tested often.
Worked Example
Find fx, fy, and fxy for f(x,y) = x³y² + sin(xy).
1
Differentiate w.r.t. x, treating y as constant (chain rule on sin(xy)):
fx = 3x²y² + y cos(xy)
2
Differentiate w.r.t. y, treating x as constant:
fy = 2x³y + x cos(xy)
3
fxy = ∂/∂y of fx (product rule on y cos(xy)):
fxy = 6x²y + cos(xy) − xy sin(xy)
fx = 3x²y²+y cos(xy),   fy = 2x³y+x cos(xy),   fxy = 6x²y+cos(xy)−xy sin(xy)
Check your work with Clairaut
Differentiate fy with respect to x instead and confirm you land on the same fyx = fxy — a great self-check on any partials problem.
Worked Example 2 — Implicit partial differentiation and higher-order partials
For f(x,y,z) = x²yz + sin(yz), a function of three variables, find fx, fy, fz, and fzz.
1

2

fy = x²z + z cos(yz)
3

fz = x²y + y cos(yz)
4
Differentiate fz again w.r.t. z (product rule on y cos(yz)):
fzz = −y² sin(yz)
fx=2xyz,   fy=x²z+z cos(yz),   fz=x²y+y cos(yz),   fzz=−y²sin(yz)

Tangent Planes and Linear Approximations

The 3D analogue of a tangent line: the flat plane that best hugs a surface at a point, built directly from the partial derivatives there.

Recall from 12.5 that a plane is fully determined by a point plus a normal vector. The tangent plane equation here is really that same idea, specialized to a surface z=f(x,y): the two tangent-line slopes fx(a,b) and fy(a,b) each describe how z changes in the x- and y-directions right at that point, and together they pin down the unique plane that best matches the surface's behavior in every direction at (a,b) — not just along the two axis directions, but in every direction, which is exactly the content of differentiability.

The linearization L(x,y) plays the same role here that the tangent-line approximation f(a)+f′(a)(x−a) played in Calc I: it's the best flat (linear) stand-in for a curved function, valid for (x,y) close to (a,b). Since a plane is much easier to evaluate than most functions, linearization gives you a fast way to estimate f(x,y) near a known point without computing f directly — genuinely useful when f is expensive or awkward to evaluate exactly, and it's exactly what you're implicitly using anytime you round a nearby measurement to a nearby "nice" point and adjust.

Tangent plane to z = f(x,y) at (x₀,y₀,z₀)
z − z₀ = fx(x₀,y₀)(x−x₀) + fy(x₀,y₀)(y−y₀)
Linearization (linear approximation)
L(x,y) = f(a,b) + fx(a,b)(x−a) + fy(a,b)(y−b)
The right-hand side of the tangent plane equation, solved for z — use it to estimate f near (a,b) without recomputing f directly.
Total differential
dz = fx(x,y) dx + fy(x,y) dy
Estimates the change Δz for small changes dx, dy in x and y.

f is called differentiable at (a,b) if Δz can be written as fxΔx + fyΔy + ε₁Δx + ε₂Δy where ε₁, ε₂ → 0 as (Δx,Δy) → (0,0). In practice: if fx and fy exist and are continuous near (a,b), f is differentiable there — this covers essentially every function you'll see in this course.

Worked Example
Find the tangent plane to z = x²+y² at (1, 2, 5), then use linearization to estimate f(1.02, 1.97).
1
Partials: fx = 2x, fy = 2y. At (1,2):

2
Tangent plane:
z − 5 = 2(x−1) + 4(y−2) ⟹ z = 2x + 4y − 5
3
Plug (1.02, 1.97) into L(x,y) = z from the plane:
L(1.02, 1.97) = 2(1.02)+4(1.97)−5 = 2.04+7.88−5
Tangent plane: z = 2x+4y−5. Estimate f(1.02,1.97) ≈ 4.92 (true value: 1.02²+1.97² = 4.9213 — very close!)
Worked Example 2 — Using the differential to estimate error
The dimensions of a closed rectangular box are measured as x=75 cm, y=60 cm, z=40 cm, each with a possible error of at most 0.2 cm. Use differentials to estimate the maximum error in the calculated volume V = xyz.

3

dV = 480 + 600 + 900 = 1980

The Chain Rule

When x and y are themselves functions of other variables, you need to add up every path of dependency — that's the multivariable chain rule.

The one-variable chain rule you already know, dz/dt = (dz/dx)(dx/dt), only has one "path" for the dependency to flow through: t affects x, which affects z. In the multivariable case, t might affect z through several different routes at once — through x and through y independently — and the chain rule says you have to account for every route and add the contributions together. That's exactly what Case 1's formula does: one term for "the x-route," one term for "the y-route," summed.

Case 2 is the same idea with more routes still: now s and t both potentially reach z through both x and y, giving four elementary derivatives (∂x/∂s, ∂y/∂s, ∂x/∂t, ∂y/∂t) combined with the two partials of z. If you ever face a chain rule problem with even more layers (say w depends on x,y,z, and those depend on r,s), the tree-diagram method generalizes without any new ideas — just more branches to multiply and sum.

Case 1: z = f(x,y), x = g(t), y = h(t)
dz/dt = (∂z/∂x)(dx/dt) + (∂z/∂y)(dy/dt)
Case 2: z = f(x,y), x = g(s,t), y = h(s,t)
∂z/∂s = (∂z/∂x)(∂x/∂s) + (∂z/∂y)(∂y/∂s)
∂z/∂t = (∂z/∂x)(∂x/∂t) + (∂z/∂y)(∂y/∂t)
Implicit differentiation via the chain rule
If F(x,y)=0 defines y implicitly: dy/dx = −Fx/Fy
If F(x,y,z)=0 defines z implicitly: ∂z/∂x = −Fx/Fz,   ∂z/∂y = −Fy/Fz
Drawing a tree diagram
Draw z at the top branching to x and y, each branching further to whatever they depend on. To find a derivative, multiply along each full branch from z down to the target variable, then add up all the branches — this never fails and avoids missing terms.

Directional Derivatives and the Gradient Vector

fx and fy only measure change along the axes. The directional derivative measures the rate of change in any direction — and the gradient tells you which direction is steepest.

Think of fx as "the special case of Duf when u = i" and fy as "the special case when u = j" — the directional derivative simply generalizes these two axis-aligned rates of change into a rate of change along any unit direction u = ⟨a,b⟩. The formula Duf = fxa + fyb is itself just an application of the Case 1 chain rule from 14.5: if you walk along the line x=x₀+at, y=y₀+bt, then d/dt f(x,y) = fx(dx/dt)+fy(dy/dt) = fxa+fyb, which is exactly the directional derivative in that direction.

The gradient's "steepest ascent" property follows straight from the dot-product formula Duf = ∇f · u = |∇f||u|cosθ = |∇f|cosθ (since u is a unit vector). This quantity is maximized exactly when cosθ = 1, i.e. when u points in the same direction as ∇f — and the maximum value achieved is |∇f|. This single fact — that ∇f always points in the direction of fastest increase, with the magnitude of the fastest rate — is arguably the most important idea in this section, and it's the reason gradient-based methods (in optimization, machine learning, physics, everywhere) walk "uphill" by stepping in the direction of ∇f.

Directional derivative
Duf(x,y) = fx(x,y) a + fy(x,y) b
where u = ⟨a,b⟩ is a unit vector giving the direction. If you're given a non-unit direction vector, normalize it first!
Gradient vector
∇f(x,y) = ⟨fx(x,y), fy(x,y)⟩
∇f(x,y,z) = ⟨fx, fy, fz⟩
Directional derivative via gradient
Duf = ∇f · u
Key facts about ∇f
Max rate of increase = |∇f|, in the direction of ∇f
Min rate (steepest decrease) = −|∇f|, direction −∇f
∇f is orthogonal to the level curve/surface through that point
Tangent planes to level surfaces
Since ∇F(x₀,y₀,z₀) is normal to the level surface F(x,y,z)=k, the tangent plane there is Fx(x−x₀)+Fy(y−y₀)+Fz(z−z₀)=0 — the same normal-vector idea from 12.5, now generated by a gradient instead of a cross product.
Worked Example

1
Gradient:
∇f = ⟨2xy³, 3x²y²−4⟩ ⟹ ∇f(2,−1) = ⟨2(2)(−1), 3(4)(1)−4⟩ = ⟨−4, 8⟩

Normalize v:
|v|=√29,   u = ⟨2/√29, 5/√29⟩
3

Duf = (−4)(2/√29) + (8)(5/√29) = (−8+40)/√29 = 32/√29
Duf(2,−1) = 32/√29. Steepest increase is in the direction ⟨−4, 8⟩ (i.e., ∇f itself), with rate |∇f| = √80 = 4√5.

Maximum and Minimum Values

Optimization in two variables: find the critical points, then use a determinant test to classify each one as a max, min, or saddle.

Why must both partials vanish at a local max or min? Picture standing at the very top of a hill on the surface z=f(x,y). If you walk in the x-direction, you're momentarily at a single-variable max, so fx=0 there (ordinary Calc I logic, applied along that slice). The same argument applies walking in the y-direction, forcing fy=0 too. So both partials must be zero simultaneously — that's the definition of a critical point, and it's a necessary condition for an extremum, exactly analogous to f′(x)=0 in one variable.

But in two variables, a new possibility opens up that never occurs in one: a saddle point, where the surface curves upward in one direction and downward in another (think of the middle of a Pringle chip, or a mountain pass — up toward the peaks on either side, down toward the valleys on the other two sides). This is exactly why the single-variable "check the sign of f″" test isn't enough anymore — you need the Second Derivatives Test, D = fxxfyy − (fxy)², to detect this new case. Intuitively, D measures whether the surface curves the same way in every direction (D>0, giving a genuine max or min) or opposite ways in different directions (D<0, a saddle).

Critical points
fx(a,b) = 0  and  fy(a,b) = 0
(or one of them doesn't exist)
Local max/min values can only occur at critical points — but not every critical point is one (saddle points).
Second Derivatives Test
D = fxx(a,b) fyy(a,b) − [fxy(a,b)]²
D > 0 and fxx(a,b) > 0 → local minimum
D > 0 and fxx(a,b) < 0 → local maximum
D < 0 → saddle point (neither max nor min)
D = 0 → test is inconclusive
Extreme Value Theorem (closed, bounded region)
Check: (1) critical points inside D, (2) f restricted to the boundary of D
Absolute max/min on a closed bounded region occurs either at an interior critical point or somewhere on the boundary — parametrize the boundary and reduce to a single-variable problem.

Double Integrals over Rectangles

Just as a single integral computes area under a curve, a double integral computes volume under a surface — built the same way, from a Riemann sum.

Recall how the single-variable definite integral was built: chop [a,b] into n small subintervals of width Δx, evaluate f at a sample point in each one, multiply by Δx to get a thin rectangle's area, and add up all the rectangles. As n→∞, this Riemann sum converges to the exact area. The double integral does the exact same thing one dimension up: chop the rectangle R into a grid of small sub-rectangles each with area ΔA = Δx·Δy, evaluate f at a sample point in each cell, multiply by ΔA to get the volume of a thin rectangular column standing on that cell, and add up all the columns. This is precisely the "volume under a surface" picture — you're filling the solid region between R and the surface z=f(x,y) with skinny boxes and adding their volumes.

Fubini's Theorem is the computational payoff of this section: rather than ever computing a genuine 2D limit of sums, you're guaranteed you can evaluate a double integral as two ordinary, sequential single-variable integrals — first integrate with respect to one variable (treating the other as a constant, exactly like a partial derivative but in reverse), then integrate the result with respect to the remaining variable. The "inside-out" order (innermost differential first) is exactly the same convention used for triple integrals in Chapter 15.6 and beyond, so it's worth getting comfortable with it now.

Double integral over rectangle R = [a,b]×[c,d]
∬R f(x,y) dA = lim Σ f(xij*, yij*) ΔA
When f(x,y) ≥ 0, this equals the volume between the surface z=f(x,y) and R.
Fubini's Theorem
∬R f(x,y) dA = ∫ₐᵇ∫cd f(x,y) dy dx = ∫cd∫ₐᵇ f(x,y) dx dy
Over a plain rectangle, order of integration doesn't matter — evaluate as two ordinary single-variable integrals, inside out.
Separable integrand
If f(x,y)=g(x)h(y):   ∬R f dA = [∫ₐᵇ g(x)dx][∫cd h(y)dy]

Double Integrals over General Regions

Most interesting regions aren't rectangles — the trick is setting up the correct bounds, which depend on the order of integration.

The whole difficulty of this section is bounds, not integration technique — once your bounds are correct, evaluating the resulting iterated integral is exactly the same skill from 15.1. What's new is that the inner integral's bounds are now allowed to depend on the outer variable (functions of x, or functions of y), rather than being fixed numbers. The rule of thumb: the outer limits of integration must always be plain numbers (constants); the inner limits are allowed to be functions of the outer variable, but never the other way around.

A Type I region is one you can describe by "sweep a vertical strip from x=a to x=b; within each strip, y runs from a bottom curve g₁(x) up to a top curve g₂(x)." A Type II region is the mirror image — horizontal strips instead. Some regions are naturally both types (a rectangle, or a disk); some are much easier to describe as one type than the other; and some regions genuinely need to be split into pieces and treated as a Type I region on one piece, Type II (or a different Type I) on another. Always start by sketching the region — the bounds essentially read themselves off a good sketch.

Type I region: a ≤ x ≤ b, g₁(x) ≤ y ≤ g₂(x)
∬D f dA = ∫ₐᵇ ∫g₁(x)g₂(x) f(x,y) dy dx
Bounded left/right by vertical lines, top/bottom by functions of x.
Type II region: c ≤ y ≤ d, h₁(y) ≤ x ≤ h₂(y)
∬D f dA = ∫cd ∫h₁(y)h₂(y) f(x,y) dx dy
Bounded top/bottom by horizontal lines, left/right by functions of y.
Area of D
A(D) = ∬D 1 dA

Double Integrals in Polar Coordinates

When a region is circular or wedge-shaped, converting to polar coordinates turns an ugly integral into a manageable one — but don't forget the extra factor of r.

Where does the extra r come from? In Cartesian coordinates, a tiny piece of area dA is a rectangle: dx times dy. In polar coordinates, the natural "grid cells" aren't rectangles — they're tiny curved wedges, bounded by two circles of radii r and r+dr, and two rays at angles θ and θ+dθ. That little wedge has an approximate area of (r dθ)(dr) — the "radial thickness" dr times the "arc length" r dθ traced out at radius r. That extra factor of r in "r dθ" is precisely where dA = r dr dθ comes from: the farther out you are from the origin, the more area a given angular slice sweeps out, so cells at large r contribute proportionally more area than cells at small r for the same dr, dθ.

Practically, converting a Cartesian double integral to polar is a three-part substitution: replace x with r cosθ, replace y with r sinθ (and simplify — x²+y² collapses beautifully to r²), and replace dA with r dr dθ (never forget this last piece — it is not just "dr dθ"). Then re-describe the region's boundary curves in terms of r and θ instead of x and y.

Conversion
x = r cos θ   y = r sin θ   x²+y² = r²

The extra factor of r comes from dA = r dr dθ — it is the single most common mistake to forget it.

Triple Integrals

Add a third dimension to the double integral and you get triple integrals — the natural tool for mass, volume, and averages over solid regions.

The conceptual jump from double to triple integrals is small — you're chopping a solid region E into tiny boxes of volume dV = dx dy dz (instead of chopping a flat region into tiny rectangles of area dA), evaluating f at a sample point in each box, and summing f · dV over every box. Just like Fubini's Theorem let you peel a double integral apart into two ordinary single-variable integrals, the same theorem lets you peel a triple integral apart into three, done one variable at a time, from the inside out.

Setting up bounds for a "Type 1" solid region (the most common case) follows a clean three-step recipe: (1) figure out the surfaces that bound the solid above and below in the z-direction — these become your z-bounds, and they're allowed to be functions of both x and y; (2) imagine flattening the solid straight down onto the xy-plane (its "shadow") — that flat region is D; (3) set up D's bounds exactly the way you did in 15.2, as an ordinary 2D double integral. The z-integral always goes first (innermost); the x- and y-integrals wrap around it exactly as they would for any double integral over D.

Over a box B = [a,b]×[c,d]×[r,s]
∭B f dV = ∫ₐᵇ∫cd∫rs f(x,y,z) dz dy dx
By Fubini, integrate in any order over a box.
General (Type 1) solid region: E between z=u₁(x,y) and z=u₂(x,y) over a region D in the xy-plane
∭E f dV = ∬D [ ∫u₁(x,y)u₂(x,y) f(x,y,z) dz ] dA
Do the z-integral first (bounds are surfaces), then finish with a 2D double integral over the "shadow" region D.
Volume of E
V(E) = ∭E 1 dV
Setting up the bounds

Triple Integrals in Cylindrical Coordinates

Cylindrical coordinates are just polar coordinates in the xy-plane with z left alone — ideal for solids with circular cross-sections.

There's genuinely nothing new to learn here beyond what you already know from 15.3 and 15.6 combined: take the polar substitution (x=r cosθ, y=r sinθ, dA=r dr dθ) and simply carry it straight into a triple integral, leaving z completely untouched. The extra factor of r shows up in dV for exactly the same reason it showed up in dA back in 15.3 — you're still slicing the region into thin wedge-shaped pieces in the xy-direction, just stacking them up through a range of z values now instead of treating them as flat.

The setup recipe is a direct extension of the Type 1 recipe from 15.6: find the z-bounds first (these can now be functions of r and θ, via x²+y²=r²), then find the "shadow" region D in the xy-plane and describe that in polar coordinates (r and θ bounds), exactly as in 15.3. When you see a cylinder, cone, or paraboloid whose cross-sections perpendicular to the z-axis are circles, that's your cue to reach for cylindrical coordinates.

Triple Integrals in Spherical Coordinates

The natural coordinate system for spheres, cones through the origin, and anything with radial symmetry in 3D.

Spherical coordinates locate a point by three genuinely different kinds of measurement: ρ (rho) is straight-line distance from the origin (always ≥ 0), φ (phi) is the angle measured down from the positive z-axis (ranging 0 to π — think of it like "latitude measured from the north pole" rather than from the equator), and θ is the same angle as in polar/cylindrical, measured around the z-axis. If you've ever seen a globe's latitude/longitude system, φ is closely related to latitude (just measured from the pole instead of the equator) and θ is exactly longitude.

The volume element dV = ρ² sinφ dρ dφ dθ is the one formula in this whole chapter most worth memorizing cold, since it's easy to lose either the ρ² or the sinφ under exam pressure. Here's the geometric reason both factors appear: imagine a tiny "box" built from a small change dρ in radius, dφ in the polar angle, and dθ in the azimuthal angle. Its three edge lengths are approximately dρ (radial), ρ dφ (an arc at radius ρ, exactly like arc length = radius × angle), and ρ sinφ dθ (an arc around the z-axis, but at the smaller "horizontal radius" ρ sinφ, not the full ρ — picture how much smaller a circle of latitude gets near the poles of a globe than at the equator). Multiply the three edge lengths together: dρ · (ρ dφ) · (ρ sinφ dθ) = ρ² sinφ dρ dφ dθ.

Since sinφ ≥ 0 for the entire valid range 0≤φ≤π, you never need absolute value bars around it — one fewer thing to worry about compared to some other volume-element derivations.

Vector Fields

A vector field assigns a vector — not a number — to every point in space: think wind velocity, gravitational pull, or fluid flow.

This is a genuine shift in what "function" means for the rest of the chapter. Up through Chapter 15, every function you integrated was scalar-valued (a plain number out). From here forward, F is vector-valued — it takes in a point and hands back an arrow, not a number. This is exactly the setup real physical fields come in: at every point in a room, the wind has both a speed and a direction; at every point near a magnet, the magnetic field has both a strength and a direction. A vector field is the mathematical object built to describe exactly that.

It's worth explicitly connecting this section back to 14.6: the gradient ∇f of any scalar function f is itself a vector field — at every point (x,y), ∇f(x,y) is a specific vector. This is why gradient fields get special attention here: they're the most important source of examples, and (as you'll see in 16.3) they have a uniquely nice property — line integrals through them only depend on the start and end points, not the path taken.

Definition
F(x,y) = P(x,y) i + Q(x,y) j     F(x,y,z) = P i + Q j + R k
Visualize by drawing the vector F(x,y) with its tail at the point (x,y) — you get an "arrow field" showing direction and relative magnitude everywhere.
Gradient fields
F = ∇f
If F is the gradient of some scalar function f, F is called a conservative vector field and f is its potential function. Every gradient field is conservative by definition — the real test for whether a given F is conservative comes in section 16.3.

Line Integrals

Instead of integrating over an interval, we now integrate along a curve — summing up a scalar function's values, or a vector field's push, along the path.

Think back to how arc length worked in 13.3: L = ∫|r′(t)|dt summed up tiny bits of length along a curve. A scalar line integral ∫C f ds does almost the same thing, except now you weight each tiny bit of arc length ds by the value of f at that point before summing — so instead of just measuring total length, you're computing something like "total f-ness accumulated along the path" (mass, if f is a linear density function; total temperature experienced, if f is a temperature field, etc.).

The vector line integral ∫C F · dr is a different, arguably more important idea: physically, it's work — the total effort a force field F exerts on a particle as it's dragged along the path C. At each instant, only the component of F pointing along the direction of motion actually contributes to the work (a force pushing sideways to your direction of travel does nothing useful) — which is exactly what the dot product F·r′(t) captures, echoing the same "dot product measures alignment" idea from 12.3.

This is also why the two line integrals behave so differently under reversing direction: ∫C f ds (scalar) doesn't care which way you walk the curve — the arc length ds is always positive regardless of direction, so the total accumulation of f is the same either way. But ∫C F·dr (vector) flips sign if you reverse direction, because reversing r′(t) reverses the sign of every dot product F·r′(t) — walking backward through a force field does the exact opposite work.

Line integral of a scalar function f, along r(t), a≤t≤b
∫C f(x,y,z) ds = ∫ₐᵇ f(r(t)) |r′(t)| dt
ds = |r′(t)| dt is the "arc-length" differential — this integral doesn't depend on the direction C is traversed.
Line integral of a vector field F along C
∫C F · dr = ∫ₐᵇ F(r(t)) · r′(t) dt = ∫C P dx + Q dy + R dz
Physically: the work done by F moving a particle along C. This one does flip sign if you reverse the direction of C.
The general setup pattern
1. Parametrize C as r(t), a≤t≤b. 2. Compute r′(t) (and |r′(t)| for a scalar integral). 3. Substitute x(t), y(t), z(t) into the integrand. 4. Reduce to an ordinary single-variable integral in t.

The Fundamental Theorem for Line Integrals

For a conservative field, the line integral only cares about the endpoints — the path itself is irrelevant. This is the multivariable version of the FTC.

Compare this theorem to the ordinary FTC from Calc I: ∫ₐᵇ f′(x)dx = f(b) − f(a) — you evaluate the antiderivative at the endpoints and subtract, completely ignoring how f′ behaved in between. The Fundamental Theorem for Line Integrals says exactly the same thing, one level up: if F happens to be a gradient field (F = ∇f), then ∫C F·dr = f(endpoint) − f(startpoint), no matter which path C takes to get from one to the other, and no matter how twisted that path is. f plays the role of "the antiderivative"; F=∇f is "the derivative."

Why does path-independence follow from conservativeness? Picture two different paths, C₁ and C₂, both running from point A to point B. If you traverse C₁ forward and then C₂ backward, you get a closed loop. If F is conservative, ∮ F·dr = 0 around any closed loop (this is essentially the theorem applied to a loop, where start=end so f(end)−f(start)=0). But ∮ = ∫C₁ − ∫C₂ (forward on one, backward — hence a sign flip — on the other), so ∫C₁ = ∫C₂: the two paths must give the same value. This chain of reasoning is exactly how Stewart's text proves the equivalence of "conservative," "path-independent," and "zero circulation around every closed loop."

The component test (∂P/∂y = ∂Q/∂x) is the practical shortcut you'll use constantly: rather than trying to guess a potential function right away, check this equation first — if it fails, F definitely isn't conservative and you can stop; if it holds (and the domain is simply-connected, meaning it has no holes), you're guaranteed a potential function exists, and you can go find it using the systematic "integrate P, then match against Q" method shown below.

Fundamental Theorem for Line Integrals
∫C ∇f · dr = f(r(b)) − f(r(a))
If F = ∇f, evaluate the line integral by just plugging endpoints into f — no parametrization needed.
Path independence & conservative fields
∫C F·dr is path-independent  ⟺  F is conservative  ⟺  ∮C F·dr = 0 for every closed curve C
Component test (for F = P i + Q j, on a simply-connected domain)
F conservative  ⟺  ∂P/∂y = ∂Q/∂x
Quick test before hunting for a potential function. In 3D the analogous condition is curl F = 0 (section 16.5).
Worked Example
Show F(x,y) = ⟨2xy, x²+3y²⟩ is conservative, find a potential f, then evaluate ∫C F·dr from (0,0) to (1,2).
1
Test: P=2xy, Q=x²+3y².
∂P/∂y = 2x  =  ∂Q/∂x = 2x  ✓ conservative
2
Integrate P w.r.t. x:
f = ∫2xy dx = x²y + g(y)
3
Differentiate w.r.t. y and match to Q:
fy = x² + g′(y) = x²+3y² ⟹ g′(y)=3y² ⟹ g(y)=y³
4
So f(x,y) = x²y + y³. Apply the FTC for line integrals:
∫CF·dr = f(1,2) − f(0,0) = (1·2+8) − 0
f(x,y) = x²y + y³; ∫C F·dr = 10
Worked Example 2 — Finding a potential function in 3D
Show F(x,y,z) = ⟨yz, xz, xy⟩ is conservative and find a potential function f.
1
Integrate P = yz with respect to x (treating y,z as constant):
f = ∫ yz  dx = xyz + g(y,z)
2
Differentiate this f with respect to y and match to Q = xz:
fy = xz + gy(y,z) = xz  ⟹  gy = 0  ⟹  g(y,z) = h(z)
3
Differentiate f = xyz + h(z) with respect to z and match to R = xy:
fz = xy + h′(z) = xy  ⟹  h′(z) = 0  ⟹  h(z) = C
f(x,y,z) = xyz (+C) — and since we found a valid potential function, F is indeed conservative (this is really the same "guess, then confirm by differentiating back" logic as 2D, just with one more variable to track).

Green's Theorem

The first of three "big theorem" upgrades: it converts a line integral around a closed curve into a double integral over the region it encloses.

Green's Theorem is the two-dimensional ancestor of both Stokes' Theorem (16.8) and the Divergence Theorem (16.9) — genuinely understanding it now will make those two much easier later, since they're built on the exact same idea, just lifted into three dimensions. The theorem trades a 1D integral (around the boundary curve C) for a 2D integral (over the whole enclosed region D) — and the quantity ∂Q/∂x − ∂P/∂y that shows up inside the double integral is precisely the 2D version of "curl," measuring the field's local rotation at each point of D. Loosely: Green's Theorem says "total rotation around the outside boundary equals the sum of all the tiny local rotations happening throughout the interior" — the little rotations in adjacent interior cells cancel each other out except right along the boundary.

Positive orientation means C is traversed so that D is always on your left as you walk along it — for a simple closed curve like a circle or a simple polygon, this just means counterclockwise. If a problem gives you a clockwise curve, either negate your final answer or explicitly reverse the curve's direction before applying the theorem.

Green's Theorem
∮C P dx + Q dy = ∬D (∂Q/∂x − ∂P/∂y) dA
C is a positively oriented (counterclockwise), piecewise-smooth, simple (non-self-intersecting) closed curve, D is the region it encloses.
Area via Green's Theorem
A = ∮C x dy = −∮C y dx = ½∮C (x dy − y dx)
Handy when the boundary is easy to parametrize but the region itself is awkward to integrate directly.
When to reach for Green's Theorem
Whenever you see a line integral over a closed curve, especially if the curve is piecewise (like a square or triangle boundary) — computing ∂Q/∂x − ∂P/∂y and one double integral is almost always faster than adding up several line-integral pieces.
Worked Example
Use Green's Theorem to evaluate ∮C (y² dx + 3xy dy), where C is the boundary of the region between y=0 and y=√(4−x²) (upper semicircle radius 2), oriented counterclockwise.
1
Identify P = y², Q = 3xy:
∂Q/∂x = 3y,   ∂P/∂y = 2y  ⟹  ∂Q/∂x−∂P/∂y = y
2
D is the upper half-disk of radius 2. Convert to polar:
∬D y dA = ∫₀^π ∫₀² (r sinθ) r dr dθ
3
Inner integral:
∫₀² r² dr = 8/3
4
Outer integral:
(8/3)∫₀^π sinθ dθ = (8/3)(2)
∮C y²dx + 3xy dy = 16/3
Worked Example 2 — Green's Theorem for a piecewise boundary
Use Green's Theorem to evaluate ∮C (x²−y) dx + (x+y²) dy, where C is the boundary (counterclockwise) of the triangle with vertices (0,0), (1,0), (0,1).
1
The boundary is three separate line segments — computing three individual line integrals directly would be tedious. Instead, identify P = x²−y, Q = x+y²:
∂Q/∂x = 1,   ∂P/∂y = −1  ⟹  ∂Q/∂x − ∂P/∂y = 2
2
Set up the double integral over the triangle D: 0≤x≤1, 0≤y≤1−x:
∬D 2  dA = 2 · (Area of D)
3
The triangle has legs of length 1, so its area is ½:
∮CF·dr = 2 · (1/2)
∮C (x²−y)dx + (x+y²)dy = 1 — notice how much faster this was than parametrizing and integrating three separate segments.

Curl and Divergence

Two operations on a vector field that summarize how it's spinning (curl) and how it's spreading out (divergence) — both built from ∇, the "del" operator.

The notational trick of writing curl F as ∇ × F and div F as ∇ · F is more than cute shorthand — it's genuinely useful for remembering which operation produces what kind of output. ∇ behaves formally like a vector ⟨∂/∂x, ∂/∂y, ∂/∂z⟩ of "operators" instead of numbers. Crossing it with a vector field F (via the same determinant recipe from 12.4) produces another vector — that's curl. Dotting it with F (component-by-component multiply-and-add, just like 12.3) produces a scalar — that's divergence. Same mnemonic as "dot → number, cross → vector" from Chapter 12, just now with derivatives mixed in.

Physical meaning matters here, not just the formula. If F represents the velocity field of a fluid, curl F at a point tells you the axis and speed a tiny paddle wheel placed there would spin around — curl F = 0 means the fluid has no local rotation anywhere (it might still be moving, just without swirling). div F at a point tells you whether fluid is being created (source, div F > 0) or destroyed (sink, div F < 0) right there, or whether it's simply flowing through without accumulating (div F = 0, called incompressible).

The two identities div(curl F) = 0 and curl(∇f) = 0 are worth internalizing rather than just memorizing, because they come up constantly as sanity checks: if you're told a field is a gradient field and you compute a nonzero curl, you've made an arithmetic mistake somewhere; if you're asked whether some vector field G could be the curl of another field and div G ≠ 0, the answer is immediately "no," without any further work.

The del operator
∇ = ⟨∂/∂x, ∂/∂y, ∂/∂z⟩
Curl (F = P i + Q j + R k)
curl F = ∇ × F = | i j k ; ∂/∂x ∂/∂y ∂/∂z ; P Q R |
= (Ry−Qz) i − (Rx−Pz) j + (Qx−Py) k
curl F is a vector. It measures the field's tendency to rotate a small paddle wheel placed at a point.
Divergence
div F = ∇ · F = Px + Qy + Rz
div F is a scalar. It measures net outward flow ("source" if positive, "sink" if negative) at a point.
Conservative field test in ℝ³
F is conservative on a simply-connected domain  ⟺  curl F = 0
Also: div(curl F) = 0 always — curl of any gradient is always the zero vector, and divergence of any curl is always 0. Both are good identities to know for spotting a setup mistake.
Worked Example
Find curl F and div F for F(x,y,z) = ⟨xy, yz, xz⟩.
1

Ry − Qz = 0 − y = −y
2
j-component (with the negative sign already applied):
−(Rx−Pz) = −(z − 0) = −z
3
k-component:
Qx − Py = 0 − x = −x
4
Divergence:
Px+Qy+Rz = y + z + x
curl F = ⟨−y, −z, −x⟩;   div F = x + y + z
Worked Example 2 — Verifying the identity div(curl F) = 0

1
Identify P=x²y, Q=y²z, R=z²x. Curl's components:
i: Ry−Qz = 0 − y² = −y²    j: −(Rx−Pz) = −(z² − 0) = −z²    k: Qx−Py = 0 − x² = −x²
2
So curl F = ⟨−y², −z², −x²⟩. Now take the divergence of this new vector field:
div(curl F) = ∂(−y²)/∂x + ∂(−z²)/∂y + ∂(−x²)/∂z

Each of these three partials is zero, since none of the three components actually depends on the variable being differentiated:
= 0 + 0 + 0

Parametric Surfaces and Their Areas

Just as r(t) traces a curve with one parameter, r(u,v) sweeps out a whole surface with two parameters — setting up everything section 16.7 needs.

This is exactly the same conceptual jump as going from a single-variable function to a two-variable function back in 14.1, applied to vector functions instead of scalar ones: r(t) (one input, Chapter 13) traces a 1-dimensional object (a curve); r(u,v) (two inputs) traces a 2-dimensional object (a surface) sitting inside 3D space. Each pair (u,v) in some parameter domain D gets mapped to a specific point r(u,v) in space, and as (u,v) ranges over all of D, those points sweep out the whole surface.

The normal vector ru × rv deserves the same geometric picture you built for the cross product back in 12.4: ru is tangent to the surface in the "u-direction" (holding v fixed and varying u slightly), rv is tangent in the "v-direction," and any two tangent vectors at a point on a surface span the entire tangent plane there — so their cross product, being perpendicular to both, must be perpendicular to the whole surface. This is exactly the surface-analogue of how ∇F gave a normal vector to a level surface back in 14.6, and both facts get used side by side throughout the rest of this chapter.

The surface area formula A(S) = ∬D|ru×rv|dA is built the same way arc length was in 13.3: |ru×rv| measures how much a tiny rectangle in the (u,v) parameter domain gets "stretched" into a tiny patch of actual surface area — integrating that stretching factor over the whole parameter domain totals up the true surface area, exactly like |r′(t)| measured stretching for arc length.

Parametric surface
r(u,v) = x(u,v) i + y(u,v) j + z(u,v) k
A sphere: r(φ,θ)=⟨a sinφ cosθ, a sinφ sinθ, a cosφ⟩. A graph z=f(x,y): r(x,y)=⟨x, y, f(x,y)⟩ (the simplest case — parametrize by x and y themselves).
Tangent vectors & normal
ru = ∂r/∂u    rv = ∂r/∂v    n = ru × rv
ru × rv is normal to the surface at that point — the key building block for surface area and surface integrals.
Surface area
A(S) = ∬D |ru × rv| dA
For a graph z=f(x,y): A(S) = ∬D √(fx²+fy²+1) dA.

Surface Integrals

The surface version of a line integral: integrate a scalar function, or the flux of a vector field, across a surface instead of along a curve.

Track the pattern across the whole chapter: line integral of a scalar function (16.2) integrated f against arc length ds; surface integral of a scalar function does the same thing one dimension up, integrating f against surface area dS. Line integral of a vector field (16.2) dotted F against the tangent direction r′(t)dt to measure "push along the curve"; surface integral of a vector field (this section) dots F against the normal direction (ru×rv)dA to measure "push through the surface" — this is exactly what flux means physically: how much of a fluid (or field) is passing through S per unit time.

Orientation matters here in a way it didn't for scalar surface integrals: a surface has two sides (think of the two faces of a sheet of paper), and F·dS measures flux through the surface in the direction of whichever normal you chose. Flip the chosen normal and the flux integral simply changes sign — same idea as reversing a curve's direction in a line integral. For a closed surface (fully enclosing a solid, like a sphere or a cube's boundary), the standard convention — used throughout section 16.9 — is to orient it with the normal pointing outward.

The "graph" formula (∬D(−Pgx−Qgy+R)dA) looks like it comes out of nowhere, but it's really just F·(ru×rv) worked out explicitly for the natural parametrization r(x,y)=⟨x,y,g(x,y)⟩: computing rx×ry for this particular r always gives ⟨−gx, −gy, 1⟩ (pointing generally upward, hence "oriented upward"), and dotting F=⟨P,Q,R⟩ against that vector produces exactly −Pgx−Qgy+R.

Surface integral of a scalar function
∬S f dS = ∬D f(r(u,v)) |ru × rv| dA
For a graph z=g(x,y): ∬S f dS = ∬D f(x,y,g(x,y)) √(gx²+gy²+1) dA.
Surface integral of a vector field (flux)
∬S F · dS = ∬S F · n dS = ∬D F(r(u,v)) · (ru × rv) dA
Measures the net flow of F through S in the direction of the chosen unit normal n. S must be oriented — pick a consistent "positive side."
Flux through a graph z = g(x,y), oriented upward
∬S F · dS = ∬D (−P gx − Q gy + R) dA

Stokes' Theorem

Green's Theorem, lifted into 3D: it relates a line integral around the boundary of a surface to a surface integral of the curl over that surface.

Revisit the "Green's Theorem is total rotation" intuition from 16.4: ∮C F·dr around a flat boundary equaled ∬D (the local 2D rotation, ∂Q/∂x−∂P/∂y) summed over the interior. Stokes' Theorem says exactly the same thing for a curved, tilted surface hanging in 3D space instead of a flat region: curl F is precisely the 3D generalization of that "local rotation" quantity (in fact, curl F · n, where n is the surface's normal, reduces exactly to ∂Q/∂x−∂P/∂y when the surface happens to be flat in the xy-plane — this is literally how you can derive Green's Theorem as a special case of Stokes').

The orientation rule ("walk along C with your head pointing along the surface's chosen normal, and S stays on your left") is the 3D version of "counterclockwise" from Green's Theorem — it's often called the right-hand rule for surfaces: curl the fingers of your right hand in the direction C is traversed, and your thumb points in the direction the surface's normal must be chosen to match. Getting this orientation backward flips the sign of your final answer, so it's worth double-checking on any problem.

The most practically useful consequence of Stokes' Theorem is that ∬S curl F · dS depends only on the boundary curve C, not on which specific surface S you chose to fill it in with — so if computing the flux over one surface looks hard, you're free to swap in any other surface with the exact same boundary, as long as it's easier to work with (a flat disk is almost always the easiest choice when the boundary is a simple closed curve like a circle).

Stokes' Theorem
∮C F · dr = ∬S curl F · dS
C is the positively-oriented boundary curve of the oriented surface S. "Positively oriented" means: walk along C with the surface's normal pointing up out of your head, and S stays on your left — the right-hand rule linking C's direction to S's chosen normal.
Why it's useful
Two big shortcuts fall out of Stokes': (1) if computing ∮CF·dr directly is hard, pick any convenient surface S bounded by C (often a flat disk) and integrate curl F over that instead. (2) Since Green's Theorem is the special case where S is flat in the xy-plane, Stokes' is the natural generalization you already half-know.
Worked Example
Use Stokes' Theorem to evaluate ∮C F · dr, F = ⟨−y, x, z⟩, C the circle x²+y²=1, z=0, oriented counterclockwise viewed from above.
1
Find curl F. P=−y,Q=x,R=z:
i: Ry−Qz=0−0=0   j: −(Rx−Pz)=−(0−0)=0   k: Qx−Py=1−(−1)=2
2
So curl F = ⟨0,0,2⟩. Choose the simplest surface bounded by C: the flat disk D: x²+y²≤1 in the plane z=0, oriented upward.
3
On this flat disk, dS points in the k direction, so curl F · dS = 2 dA:
∬D 2 dA = 2 · (area of unit disk = π)
∮C F · dr = 2π

The Divergence Theorem

The last of the big three: it converts a flux integral over a closed surface into a triple integral of divergence over the solid it encloses — the 3D sibling of Green's Theorem.

Line up all three big theorems side by side and the pattern becomes obvious: Green's Theorem relates a boundary (a closed curve C) to its interior (a flat region D), via ∂Q/∂x−∂P/∂y. Stokes' Theorem relates a boundary (a closed curve C) to a surface it bounds, via curl F. The Divergence Theorem relates a boundary (a closed surface S) to the solid it encloses, via div F. Each theorem is the same fundamental idea — "the total flow/rotation across a boundary equals the sum of all the tiny local flow/rotation happening throughout the interior" — applied one dimension higher than the last.

Physically: if F is the velocity field of a fluid, div F at a point measures the local rate at which fluid is expanding (being created) or compressing (being destroyed) right there. The Divergence Theorem says the total flux out through a closed surface S — how much fluid is net leaving the enclosed region per unit time — must exactly equal the sum of all that local expansion happening throughout the interior. If nothing inside is being created or destroyed (div F = 0 everywhere, an incompressible fluid), then whatever flows in must flow back out, and net flux through any closed surface is automatically zero — no computation needed.

Practically, this theorem is often the fastest tool in the entire chapter for evaluating a flux integral, especially when S is made of multiple pieces (like a cylinder's lateral surface plus its top and bottom caps) — instead of computing three separate surface integrals with three different parametrizations and normals, you convert the whole problem into a single triple integral of div F, which is frequently much simpler algebraically.

The Divergence Theorem
∬S F · dS = ∭E div F  dV
E is a solid region, S = ∂E is its boundary surface, oriented outward. Requires S to be closed (fully enclosing E).
Why it's the workhorse of the chapter
Flux integrals over closed surfaces made of several pieces (e.g. a cylinder's side plus its top and bottom disks) are exactly what this theorem is built for: skip computing three separate surface integrals and instead evaluate one triple integral of div F, often in cylindrical or spherical coordinates.
